From the ballot box to La Moncloa

What Spain votes on in the general election of 29 November and how a ballot paper becomes a government, told with the 350 seats in its Congress.

La Moncloa is the prime minister's official residence in Madrid, and shorthand for the Spanish government, as Downing Street is for the British one.

1 · What's being elected

On Sunday 29 November, Spain elects its Congress of Deputies, the lower house of parliament and the one that chooses the prime minister. It has 350 (escaños, in Spanish), shared out by : in practice, by province.

Voters do not choose the prime minister directly, and there is no single national count: there are 52 separate contests.

More detail

Spain in brief

Spain is a parliamentary monarchy. The King is head of state, but the country is run by a government led by a prime minister, whose formal title is President of the Government (presidente del Gobierno).

Voters do not elect the prime minister. They elect a parliament, the Cortes Generales, and its lower house, the Congress of Deputies, chooses the prime minister in a vote called the investiture. That is why the number to watch is seats in Congress: 176 of the 350 is a majority.

The country is divided into 50 provinces, plus the cities of Ceuta and Melilla on the North African coast. Each one is a constituency. The provinces are grouped into 17 autonomous communities, the regions, which have their own parliaments and governments.

La Moncloa is the palace complex in Madrid where the prime minister lives and works. In Spanish politics “la Moncloa” means the government, much as “Downing Street” does in Britain.

Sources: Constitution, arts. 1.3, 56, 66, 68 and 99. The 17 autonomous communities and the note on La Moncloa are general context added for this English edition.

Why some provinces have more seats

Every province starts with two seats; Ceuta and Melilla, Spain's two cities on the North African coast, have one each. That makes 102. The other 248 are shared out by population.

It works like this: the population of all the provinces is divided by 248 to get a quota. Each province receives as many seats as the number of times that quota fits into its population. The seats left over go to the provinces that came closest to earning one more.

For this election the figures used are those of the municipal register (the padrón) at 1 January 2025, and the quota works out at about 197,000 inhabitants. That is why Madrid elects 38 deputies and Soria 2.

Sources: LOREG (the general electoral law, Organic Law 5/1985), art. 162; Royal Decree 806/2026, annex; Royal Decree 1117/2025, annex. The figure of 102 is 50 provinces times two, plus Ceuta and Melilla. The quota (197,355) is an own calculation, and it reproduces the decree's allocation exactly.

Who can vote

Spanish citizens aged 18 or over who are on the . The only people who lose the right are those sentenced to that specific penalty by a final court ruling, for as long as it lasts.

Foreign residents cannot vote in a general election, whatever their nationality.

Voting is in person, at the polling station each voter is assigned to, and only once. Voters identify themselves with their national identity card (the DNI), a passport or a photo driving licence.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 2, 3, 4 and 85.

Why 29 November

The prime minister called the election early: the decree dissolving parliament was published in the official state gazette (the BOE) on 6 October. The law sets polling day 54 days after an election is called, which falls on Sunday 29 November.

Spanish media abbreviate election dates: “29N” is 29 November, just as “23J” was the previous general election, on 23 July 2023.

If an election ever has to be repeated, the timetable is shorter: 47 days.

Sources: Constitution, art. 115; LOREG, art. 42.1 and seventh additional provision; Royal Decree 806/2026, arts. 1 and 2, published in the BOE of 6 October 2026. 29 November is an own count of the 54th day.

What voting looks like

Polling stations are open from 9am to 8pm, without a break. Each voter casts two votes: a white ballot paper for Congress and a sepia one for the Senate, each in its own envelope.

Using the booth is optional. At the table, the chair reads out the voter's name, says “vota” (“votes”) and the envelope goes into the ballot box.

The vote is secret: nobody can be made to say how they voted. Voters who cannot read or handle the ballot papers may be helped by someone they trust.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 5, 84, 86 and 87; Royal Decree 605/1999, art. 4.

Who staffs the polling station

Each (mesa electoral) is three people: a chair and two members. They are drawn by lot from among that station's voters who can read and write and are under seventy. Serving is compulsory.

When the polls close, they open the ballot box and count the votes in public, one by one and out loud.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 25, 26, 27 and 95.

Who oversees the election

The (juntas electorales). They see that the process is clean and equal for everyone, rule on complaints and appeals, police opinion polls and carry out the official count.

There is a Central Electoral Board (Junta Electoral Central) in Madrid, one in each province and others at district level. The central board is made up of eight Supreme Court judges, chosen by lot, and five professors of law, political science or sociology proposed by the parties with seats in Congress. Judges are also in the majority on the provincial and district boards.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 8, 9, 10, 11, 19, 69 and 103.

What the Cortes are and what they do

The are Spain's parliament: Congress and the Senate together. They make laws, approve the budget and hold the government to account. They are elected for four years.

Of the two chambers, Congress is the one that elects the prime minister and the one that can override the Senate's vetoes.

Sources: Constitution, arts. 66, 68.4, 69.6, 90 and 99.

How the lists work

For Congress, voters choose a party's whole , with as many names as the province has seats. The lists are closed: crossing out a name or changing the order spoils the vote.

Lists alternate women and men. Any adult Spanish citizen with the right to vote can stand, except holders of certain posts, such as serving judges, members of the armed forces and police officers.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 6, 44 bis, 46 and 96.

On the map, each hexagon is a seat: provinces grow or shrink according to the seats they elect, inside the outline of Spain. The Canary Islands are in the box.

How the seats are shared out

Each party's votes are divided by 1, 2, 3 and so on. The seats go, one at a time, to the highest results in the whole table. This is the (regla D'Hondt, in Spanish).

This is the table for the province you chose, with the example parties. In terracotta, the results that win a seat, numbered in the order they win it:

The law itself gives an example: with 480,000 votes and 8 seats, the party with most votes takes four, the second two, and the third and fourth one each.

Source: LOREG, art. 163. In the table, the made-up percentages stand in for votes.

How long it has been used

Since 1977. It was set by the royal decree-law on electoral rules of March that year, which governed the first elections after the Political Reform Act that opened the way out of the Franco dictatorship, already with 350 deputies. It already called it the “d'Hondt rule”, already set the threshold at 3% and already carried the same 480,000-vote example the law gives today.

That decree presented it as a brake on Congress becoming too fragmented. And the share-out between provinces was different then: Madrid elected 32 deputies, Barcelona 33 and Soria 3.

Source: Royal Decree-Law 20/1977 of 18 March on electoral rules: preamble and arts. 19 and 20.

The 3% threshold

Before the share-out, parties with less than 3% of the valid votes in the province are set aside. Their votes are not passed on to anyone: they simply do not enter the table.

The threshold applies province by province. There is no national threshold.

Source: LOREG, art. 163.

Blank votes, spoilt votes and abstention

A is an empty envelope. It is valid: it goes to no party, but it counts in the total and so raises the 3% bar slightly.

A does not count towards the share-out. A ballot paper with crossings-out or additions is spoilt, and so is an envelope that is not the official one. Two ballot papers for the same party in one envelope count as a single vote.

Those who do not vote abstain: they do not enter any calculation.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 96 and 108.

What happens in a tie

If two parties tie on a division, the seat goes to the one with more votes overall. If they tie on that too, lots are drawn.

Source: LOREG, art. 163.

Who fills each seat

A party's seats go to the first names on its list, in order. If a deputy resigns or dies, the next person on the same list, or a , takes over. There are no by-elections.

Sources: LOREG, arts. 163 and 164.

Ceuta and Melilla

Each elects a single deputy, so there is nothing to share out: whoever gets most votes wins.

Source: LOREG, art. 163.2.

The last seat

The last seat in each province is the most closely fought. It goes to the party with the next-highest result, and sometimes another party falls just short.

3 · Where the vote is cast

The same percentages do not produce the same result everywhere. The fewer seats a province elects, the fewer parties get in.

    How many inhabitants stand behind each seat

    Seats are shared out by population, but with a minimum of two per province. That is why far fewer inhabitants are needed to elect a deputy in the small provinces.

    In this election, Soria has one seat for every 45,117 inhabitants; Madrid, one for every 187,817: four times as many.

    The count is of inhabitants, not voters: it includes children and foreign residents, who do not vote in this election.

      What the Constitution says and what the law says

      The Constitution fixes three things: that Congress has between 300 and 400 deputies, that each province is a constituency and that the allocation is proportional.

      The rest is decided by the electoral law: that there are 350, that each province starts with two, the D'Hondt method and the 3% threshold.

      Changing the electoral law takes an absolute majority in Congress, because it is an . Changing the Constitution takes a good deal more.

      4 · The Senate

      The Senate, the upper house, is elected differently: voters mark people, not lists. In a mainland province they can mark up to three names, and the four people with most votes are elected.

      Try ticking the boxes (made-up names)

      The Senate does not elect the prime minister and cannot bring one down. But the Constitution cannot be amended without it, and it chooses some of the members of the Constitutional Court and of the General Council of the Judiciary.

      More detail

      The key to constitutional reform

      Amending the Constitution takes three-fifths of Congress and three-fifths of the Senate. If the Senate falls short of three-fifths there is another route: it approves the change by an absolute majority and Congress approves it by two-thirds. Without an absolute majority in the Senate, the reform fails.

      The most far-reaching reforms, those touching the preliminary title, fundamental rights or the Crown, require two-thirds of each chamber, the dissolution of parliament, ratification by the new chambers, again by two-thirds, and a referendum.

      In practice, whoever holds more than half the Senate can block any reform, and whoever holds more than a third can block the most far-reaching ones. In addition, a tenth of senators can demand that any reform be put to a referendum.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 167 and 168. The blocking figures are an own calculation from those majorities.

      Appointments that go through the Senate

      The Senate nominates four of the twelve judges of the Constitutional Court and elects ten of the twenty members of the General Council of the Judiciary, the body that governs the judges; Congress elects the other ten. In both cases three-fifths of senators are needed.

      That means whoever holds more than two-fifths of the Senate can block those appointments.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 122.3 and 159.1; Organic Law on the Judiciary, art. 567.2. The blocking figure is an own calculation from the three-fifths majority.

      Islands, Ceuta and Melilla

      Gran Canaria, Mallorca and Tenerife elect three senators each, and voters mark two names. Ceuta and Melilla elect two each, and voters mark two. On each of the other islands one is elected and one is marked.

      Ibiza and Formentera have elected their senators separately since the constitutional reform of May 2026.

      In all, 209 senators are elected this way.

      Sources: Royal Decree 806/2026, art. 3; LOREG, arts. 165 and 166; Constitution, art. 69. The total of 209 is an own sum from the decree.

      What the ballot paper looks like

      It is a single sepia sheet with every candidacy on it: each party with its candidates underneath and a box in front of each name. Voters put a cross against those they choose, three at most, and they can be from different parties: the law only limits how many.

      Marking more than the number allowed spoils the vote. Marking nobody makes it a blank vote.

      Sources: Royal Decree 605/1999, art. 4 and annex 3; LOREG, arts. 96, 166 and 172.3.

      What the Senate is for

      The Constitution defines it as the chamber of territorial representation. It reviews the bills that reach it from Congress: it has two months to amend or veto them. Congress can override that .

      There is one thing only the Senate can authorise, by an absolute majority: for the central government to compel an autonomous community (a region) to comply with the law. This is article 155.

      It can also propose laws, set up committees of inquiry and, with 50 senators, challenge a law before the Constitutional Court. Cooperation agreements between autonomous communities and the sharing-out of the Interterritorial Compensation Fund start in the Senate, although if the two chambers disagree, Congress decides.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 69, 74.2, 76, 87.1, 90, 145.2, 155, 158.2 and 162.1.

      What the Senate cannot do

      It does not vote on the investiture or on a motion of no confidence: those belong to Congress alone. Nor does it decide on the government's decree-laws or on states of alarm, emergency and siege.

      It can question the government every week, but it cannot bring it down.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 86, 99, 111, 113 and 116.

      If the Senate has a different majority

      If the Senate is controlled by a different majority from Congress, it can delay bills by two months and force Congress to vote on them again. And it can block constitutional reforms and the appointments that need three-fifths.

      What it cannot do is stop a law for good: Congress has the last word.

      Own explanation, based on arts. 90, 122.3, 159.1, 167 and 168 of the Constitution.

      The senators nobody votes for

      Besides those elected at the ballot box, each regional parliament appoints one senator, plus another for every million inhabitants of its autonomous community. These are the .

      If a senator elected at the ballot box gives up the seat, their substitute takes it.

      Sources: Constitution, art. 69.5; LOREG, art. 166.

      5 · Majorities

      To elect a prime minister at the first attempt, a candidate needs an : 176 yes votes out of 350. Failing that, 48 hours later a is enough: more yes votes than no votes.

      In terracotta, the 176 seats that mark half plus one.

      More detail

      Why abstaining weighs differently

      In the first vote only the yes votes count: 176 are needed, and abstaining does not help.

      In the second, yes votes are compared with no votes. There, abstaining instead of voting no does help the candidate, because there is one fewer no to beat.

      Own explanation, based on art. 99.3 of the Constitution.

      Other majorities

      An absolute majority (176) is also needed to pass organic laws and a motion of no confidence.

      Three-fifths (210): amending the Constitution and nominating judges of the Constitutional Court.

      Two-thirds (234): the most far-reaching constitutional reforms.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 81, 99, 113, 159, 167 and 168. The figures 210 and 234 are calculated on 350 deputies.

      Day to day: quorum and simple majority

      For a vote to be valid, more than half the chamber must be present: that is the . Ordinary decisions pass with more votes in favour than against among those present.

      If there is a tie, the vote is repeated. If the tie persists, there is a pause and a third vote. If it is still tied, the proposal is deemed rejected.

      Sources: Constitution, art. 79; Standing Orders of Congress, arts. 78, 79 and 88.

      Organic laws and decree-laws

      Organic laws deal with the weightiest matters: fundamental rights, the regions' statutes of autonomy or the electoral rules. They need an absolute majority in Congress.

      The is the government's shortcut for urgent cases: it passes it on its own and it takes effect, but Congress has thirty days to uphold it or strike it down.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 81 and 86.

      No-confidence motions and confidence votes

      A (moción de censura) is the way to change prime minister without an election. It must be signed by at least 35 deputies, name a candidate and win an absolute majority. If it passes, that candidate becomes prime minister: it cannot just remove one, it has to install another.

      A (cuestión de confianza) works the other way round: the prime minister calls it. A simple majority is enough. If it is lost, the prime minister resigns and a new one has to be found.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 112, 113 and 114. The 35 deputies are a tenth of 350.

      The budget

      The government drafts it and the Cortes approve it. If the year starts without a new budget, the previous year's stays in force until one is passed.

      That is why a government without a majority can carry on governing, but with another year's accounts.

      Source: Constitution, art. 134.

      Three ways of lending support

      A party can help a candidate in three ways. By joining the government: that is a coalition. By voting yes without joining the government. Or by abstaining, so that the second vote is enough.

      What is asked for in return is not in any law: it is political negotiation.

      Own explanation. The majorities are in art. 99 of the Constitution.

      How the investiture vote is held

      One by one and out loud. Each deputy is called in alphabetical order of first surname, starting from one drawn by lot, and answers “sí”, “no” or “abstención”. Members of the government who are deputies, and the Bureau, vote last.

      First comes a debate: the candidate sets out a programme with no time limit and each group replies for half an hour.

      Sources: Standing Orders of Congress, arts. 85, 86 and 171.

      If the investiture fails

      If the candidate is not elected, the King can nominate others. But the clock is already running: two months after the first vote, if there is no prime minister, parliament is dissolved and the country votes again.

      It has happened twice. In 2016 and in 2019 nobody won an investiture in time and the election was repeated.

      A repeat election moves faster: polling day is 47 days after it is called, not 54.

      Sources: Constitution, art. 99; LOREG, art. 42 and seventh additional provision (added by Organic Law 2/2016); Royal Decrees 184/2016 and 551/2019.

      Where these seat numbers come from

      They are not results: they are seats projected from the latest polls by Porra Electoral (in Spanish), which pools the published polls and simulates the share-out province by province. On 30 November they will be replaced by the real seats.

      Source: Porra Electoral.

      Why the calculator carries a date

      Spanish law bans publishing or disseminating election polls in the five days before polling day: for this election, from 24 to 28 November. That is why the estimate in this calculator was frozen beforehand and carries the date of the last poll it includes.

      The body that checks that polls comply with the law is the Central Electoral Board.

      Source: LOREG, art. 69. 24 to 28 November is an own count of the five days.

      More detail

      The campaign and the day of reflection

      The campaign lasts fifteen days: from Friday 13 to Friday 27 November. It is the period in which parties ask for votes with rallies, posters and adverts. Before the 13th they may not put up election posters or run election adverts.

      On Saturday 28, the eve of the vote, there can be no advertising or campaign events. It is known as the day of reflection (jornada de reflexión).

      Sources: LOREG, arts. 51 and 53; Royal Decree 806/2026, art. 4. “Day of reflection” is the everyday name; the law speaks of the period after the campaign has ended.

      Voting by post and from abroad

      Voters who cannot go on the day can vote by post. They must apply at a post office, in person and with their identity card, up to ten days before polling day: for this election, until 19 November. The ballot papers then arrive at home and are returned by registered post, free of charge.

      Spaniards who live abroad and are on the register of absent residents receive the paperwork at home without asking for it. They can vote in a ballot box at their consulate, from 21 to 26 November, or post their vote to the consulate by the 24th at the latest. Those votes are counted on 4 December, in the official count.

      Sources: LOREG, arts. 72, 73, 75 and 103. The dates are an own count of the deadlines in those articles for this election.

      Election night is not the official result

      On election night a provisional count is announced, based on the polling stations' tally sheets. The official one, the , is carried out by the on the fifth day: 4 December. That is when the votes from abroad are added.

      It must be finished by the eighth day at the latest: 7 December. The deputies are then proclaimed. From that moment there are three days to appeal, and appeals are decided by the Supreme Court.

      Sources: LOREG, arts. 75, 98, 103, 107, 108 and 112. 4 and 7 December are an own count of the fifth and eighth days.

      Why 23 December

      The Constitution allows 25 days from polling day to convene the new Congress. The decree that called the election set 23 December at ten o'clock.

      Sources: Constitution, art. 68.6; Royal Decree 806/2026, art. 5.

      The first day of Congress

      The oldest deputy presides, with the two youngest as secretaries. The first business is to elect the . Its president, the speaker, needs an absolute majority; if nobody gets one, the choice is between the two with most votes.

      The deputies then swear or promise to abide by the Constitution and Congress is constituted.

      To become a full deputy, a member must hand in their credentials and declaration of activities, and swear or promise to abide by the Constitution. Anyone who has not done so within three plenary sittings is left without rights or privileges until they do.

      The are formed in the following five days.

      Sources: Standing Orders of Congress, arts. 1 to 4, 20, 23, 24 and 37.

      The King's consultations

      The King receives the representatives of the parties with seats and then nominates a candidate, through the speaker of Congress. The Constitution sets no deadline.

      That is why the calendar has a stretch with no dates: until there is a first vote, the two-month clock does not run.

      Source: Constitution, arts. 62 and 99.

      Who governs in the meantime

      The outgoing government stays on until the new one takes office. It deals with day-to-day business, but it cannot present bills or the budget.

      Nor can its prime minister dissolve parliament, call a confidence vote or propose a referendum.

      Sources: Constitution, art. 101; Law 50/1997, art. 21.

      And while there is no parliament

      From the dissolution of the chambers until the new ones are constituted, a small group of deputies and another of senators stay on duty: the (Diputación Permanente).

      It does what Congress cannot do in that period: uphold or strike down the government's and decide on states of alarm, emergency and siege.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 78, 86 and 116.

      Once there is a prime minister

      The King appoints the prime minister Congress has elected. He then appoints the ministers the prime minister proposes. When the new government takes office, the previous one ceases to be a caretaker.

      Sources: Constitution, arts. 62, 99, 100 and 101.

      How to read the map

      Each hexagon is a seat, in the colour of the party that won it. Big provinces take up more room because they elect more seats, not because they have more territory.

      Provisional and official

      What is known on election night is a provisional count. The official one is carried out by the electoral boards five days later, and that is when the votes from abroad are added.

      The deputies are then proclaimed. From that moment there are three days to appeal, and appeals are decided by the Supreme Court.

      Sources: LOREG, arts. 75, 98, 103, 108 and 112.

      What valid votes are

      Percentages are calculated on valid votes: those cast for a party plus blank votes. Spoilt votes are left out.

      Sources: LOREG, arts. 96 and 108.

      10 · In one province

        What if…?

        Move votes from one party to another and see how the share-out changes in this province. The total number of votes stays the same.

          More detail

          How the shortfall is calculated

          The last seat goes to the highest result left in the table. The next party's shortfall is how many more votes it would have needed for its own result to beat that one, with nothing else changing.

          It is a calculation on paper: in reality, if one party goes up, others go down.

          Own calculation with the rule in art. 163 of the LOREG.

          If a deputy gives up the seat

          The next person on the list they were elected on takes over; there is no by-election.

          Source: LOREG, art. 164.

          11 · Votes and seats

            votes seats

            More detail

            Why they don't match

            Because the seats are not shared out once for the whole of Spain but 52 times: once per constituency. In the small ones only the biggest parties get in, and the votes for the rest win no seat.

            On top of that come the 3% threshold and the fact that the D'Hondt method rounds in favour of the parties with most votes in each province.

            Own explanation, based on arts. 162 and 163 of the LOREG.

            What would have to change for them to match

            The province as the constituency is in the Constitution. The number of deputies (350), the minimum of two per province, the threshold and the allocation formula are in the electoral law.

            The first requires amending the Constitution. The second, an absolute majority in Congress.

            Sources: Constitution, arts. 68 and 81; LOREG, arts. 162 and 163.

            12 · Who governs?

            These are the real seats. Tap each party to change how it votes in the investiture: no, yes or abstain.

            More detail

            What to watch in a negotiation

            Two numbers. If the yes votes reach 176, there is a prime minister at the first attempt. If they do not, what matters is that there are more yes votes than no votes: that is where every abstention counts.

            Source: Constitution, art. 99.3.

            Deputies are not bound

            The Constitution says deputies and senators are not bound by any compulsory mandate: nobody can legally force them to vote one way or another.

            Source: Constitution, art. 67.2.

            13 · What's next

              More detail

              If there is no deal

              Two months after the first investiture vote, if there is no prime minister, parliament is dissolved and a new election is called. It would be held 47 days later.

              It has happened twice, in 2016 and in 2019.

              Sources: Constitution, art. 99.5; LOREG, seventh additional provision (added by Organic Law 2/2016); Royal Decrees 184/2016 and 551/2019.

              If there is a deal

              The King appoints the prime minister and, on the prime minister's proposal, the ministers. The caretaker government ends when the new one takes office.

              Sources: Constitution, arts. 62, 99, 100 and 101.
